The notebook he never meant to publish

Marcus Aurelius ruled Rome from 161 to 180 and kept a notebook he never meant anyone else to read. He wrote it in Greek, to himself, partly in army camps on the Danube frontier. We know it as the Meditations.

Most English versions sound like the 1800s, because the free ones are from then. The lines below start from George Long's 1862 translation and are rewritten in the words you'd use today, with his meaning kept.

On staying calm

Calm is nothing more than a mind kept in good order.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

A calm mind is a safe stronghold. You have no better place to take refuge.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

To a worrying thought: you came in your usual way, and I'm not angry with you. Just go.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

He talks to the worry as if it were an old acquaintance at the door. That's the whole trick: not fighting it, just not inviting it in.

How easy it is to push away a troubling thought and be calm again at once.

You can live in deep calm even if the whole world shouts against you.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

How these were written

Long's translation is in the public domain, and it's accurate, but it says "thou" and "thy" and uses Stoic terms of art. We kept each line close to his meaning and changed only the words: "you" for "thou", today's verbs, and plain words where he used philosophy's. If you'd like the original wording, Long's full text is free on Project Gutenberg.

Questions

What is Marcus Aurelius's most famous quote?

Many of the best-known lines are about the mind: that things trouble us only through our view of them, and that a calm mind is a safe stronghold. In plain English: "Things just sit there, saying nothing about themselves. The judging is done by you."

What did Marcus Aurelius write?

The Meditations, a set of private notes to himself, written in Greek while he was emperor of Rome. He never meant them to be published. They were copied and kept after his death, and they are still among the most read books of philosophy.

Why are these quotes in modern English?

The free English translations date from the 1800s and use "thou" and old Stoic terms. These lines start from George Long's 1862 translation and keep its meaning in the words you'd use today.

Was Marcus Aurelius a Stoic?

Yes. He studied Stoic philosophy from his youth, and the Meditations is one of the best-known books the Stoics left behind, along with the letters of Seneca and the teachings of Epictetus.
