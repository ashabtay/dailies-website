Daily affirmations you can actually believe

Daily affirmations work best when you believe them. These are first-person lines that real writers wrote about themselves, sorted by the part of the day they suit. Pick one you would sign, then say it, write it down or keep it where you will see it.

By Amit Shabtay, who makes Dailies Published 26 November 2026

Why these are a little different

Most lists of daily affirmations ask you to say something you may not believe yet: "I am confident", "I attract success". In a 2009 study in Psychological Science, Joanne Wood and her colleagues at the University of Waterloo found that repeating "I'm a lovable person" lifted people who already felt good about themselves a little, and left people with low self-esteem feeling worse. A line you don't believe gets argued with. There's more on that in Do affirmations work?

So every affirmation here is something a real person once wrote about themselves, and most of them are modest enough to be true on an ordinary day. They're in the first person, so you can say them as your own.

For the morning

A morning affirmation doesn't have to be big. It only has to get you moving.

I'm hard to get moving; but once I'm on my way, I'll go as far as you like.

Montaigne, Essays

Montaigne, writing about himself in the 1500s, and about most of us before the first coffee.

Today you worry about tomorrow, tomorrow about eternity. I will worry today about today; tomorrow is time enough for tomorrow.

Franz Grillparzer, Poems

One day's worth of worry is plenty. The rest can wait its turn.

For confidence

Four words from Don Quixote. An affirmation doesn't need to be longer than that.

I am better than my reputation.

Friedrich Schiller, Mary Stuart

For the days when someone else's opinion of you is louder than your own.

I don't believe you have to be extraordinary to have the right to say who you are.

Anatole France, On Life and Letters

I have as much soul as anyone, and as much body as the best of them.

Miguel de Cervantes, Don Quixote

Also from Don Quixote, and a good one to keep for rooms where you feel out of place.

For work

I do my part. Other things don't trouble me.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

A good one for the start of a busy day. Your part is yours. The rest belongs to other people.

I'm slow and muddled to grasp things; but what I grasp once, I hold well.

Montaigne, Essays

For anyone learning something new and feeling slow at it.

I do nothing without cheerfulness; grinding on too long and too hard dazzles my judgment, saddens it and wears it out.

Montaigne, Essays

An affirmation that is also permission to stop before you're worn out.

For a hard day

I bend, and do not break.

Jean de La Fontaine, Fables

From the fable of the oak and the reed. The storm knocks down the proud oak, and the reed is still standing, because it bent.

I know this faintness of spirit does not last.

José Enrique Rodó, The Motives of Proteus

I have faith in myself: hard times could take the victory from me, but never the glory.

Salvador Díaz Mirón, To Gloria

For being kinder to yourself

I must put up with myself as I am, with my countless imperfections.

Thérèse of Lisieux, Story of a Soul

I have done plenty of foolish things in my life, and never a malicious one.

Montesquieu, Miscellaneous Thoughts

A generous way to look back at your own mistakes, and a true one for most people.

I care less about who I am in other people's eyes than about who I am in myself.

Montaigne, Essays

I live; that is, I change.

Enrique José Varona, With the Flint

Short enough to remember. Changing isn't failing. It's what being alive looks like.

For the evening

I stop: my walk has a full stop, like a sentence you've finished.

Paul Claudel, The East I Know

For the end of the day: a full stop, not a list of what's left.

Today I got clear of every trouble. Or rather, I threw them out, because they were never out there. They were in my opinions.

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

How to use daily affirmations

Pick one, not ten

One line you mean does more than a list you rush through. Keep it for a week, and change it when it stops fitting.

Tie it to something you already do

"When I put the kettle on, I read my line." The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer calls plans like this implementation intentions. He and Paschal Sheeran reviewed close to a hundred studies of them in 2006 and found a clear effect on whether people followed through.

Keep it where you'll see it

A note on the mirror works. So does your phone, which you'll look at anyway: here's how to put affirmations on your iPhone Lock Screen.

If it rings false, change the format

Writing for a few minutes about something you value, which psychologists call self-affirmation, has better research behind it than repeating statements about yourself. Geoffrey Cohen and David Sherman reviewed that research in the Annual Review of Psychology in 2014.

Questions

What are daily affirmations?

Short positive statements about yourself that you say, write or read every day, usually in the first person. The idea is that repeating them shapes how you see yourself.

Do daily affirmations work?

For some people. A 2009 study in Psychological Science found that repeating "I'm a lovable person" helped people with high self-esteem a little and made people with low self-esteem feel worse. Statements you can believe, and writing about what you value, have better support.

What is a good affirmation to say every day?

Something short and true. "I know who I am", from Don Quixote, is four words long. Marcus Aurelius' "I do my part. Other things don't trouble me" is a good one for a busy day.

When is the best time to say daily affirmations?

Whenever you will actually do it. Tying it to something you already do every day, like making coffee or brushing your teeth, makes it much easier to remember.
