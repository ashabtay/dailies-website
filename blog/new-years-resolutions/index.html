New Year's resolutions that actually stick

New Year's resolutions have a reputation as a setup for failure, and plenty of them are gone by the second week of January. But the research tells a more hopeful story: people who make one are far more likely to change than people who wait for a better moment. Here is what separates the resolutions that last from the ones that don't, and how to write yours so it is one of the first kind.

Are resolutions worth making at all?

Yes, and the evidence for it is older than most of the jokes. In 2002 John Norcross and his colleagues at the University of Scranton followed two groups of people who wanted to change the same kinds of things, such as losing weight, exercising more or quitting smoking. One group made a New Year's resolution; the other was interested in changing but did not make one. Six months later, 46 percent of the resolvers were still succeeding, against 4 percent of the people who had only meant to. The study was published in the Journal of Clinical Psychology.

Bells at New Year: like the mighty heartbeat of a strong, invincible hope for life.

Christian Morgenstern, Stages

The first of January works partly because it feels like a clean page. Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis called this the fresh start effect: in a 2014 paper in Management Science they found that searches for "diet" and visits to a university gym both rose at the start of a year, a month, a week, and after birthdays and holidays. A date that marks a new chapter makes it easier to put the old self's failures behind you. The new year is simply the biggest of those dates.

Aim toward something, not away from it

The largest study of resolutions so far, published in PLOS ONE in 2020 by Martin Oscarsson and colleagues at Stockholm University, followed more than a thousand people for a year. Its clearest finding was about wording. People whose resolution was to do something, an approach goal like "start running twice a week", were more successful than people whose resolution was to stop something, an avoidance goal like "stop eating sweets": 58.9 percent against 47.1 percent after a year.

An avoidance goal gives you nothing to do, only something to not do, and every slip is a failure. An approach goal gives you an action, and every time you take it is a small win. So turn "stop scrolling in bed" into "read ten pages in bed". Same aim, better odds.

Start smaller than feels worth it

The classic resolution is enormous: get fit, write the book, fix the money. It fails less because it is wrong than because it asks for a new life starting on a Wednesday.

We start out so fast that we run out of breath halfway.

Saint-Évremond, Ideas and Maxims

Pick the smallest version you could do on a bad day, and do that. A ten minute walk. One page. One paid bill. Size it up once it has become automatic, which usually takes longer than the three weeks people quote: our post on how long it takes to form a habit has what the research found.

Every beginning is small.

Joseph Joubert, Thoughts, Essays and Maxims

Decide when and where, in advance

"Exercise more" is a wish. "After I drop the kids at school on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, I walk for twenty minutes before going home" is a plan. Peter Gollwitzer, a psychologist at New York University, calls this an implementation intention: an if-then plan that ties the action to a moment that already happens. In a 2006 review of 94 studies by Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran, people who made one were markedly more likely to follow through than people who only set the goal.

The plan works because the decision is already made before the moment arrives. Write yours down, as a sentence, with the when and the where in it. More on this in how to stop procrastinating.

Make it pleasant now, not just good for you later

Kaitlin Woolley and Ayelet Fishbach, at the University of Chicago, asked people about their New Year's resolutions and checked back weeks later. Whether people were still at it was predicted by how much they enjoyed the activity day to day, not by how important they thought it was; the study appeared in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin in 2017. The reasons we make a resolution are long-term. The reasons we keep one are immediate.

So choose the version you like. The exercise you look forward to beats the one that is theoretically best. Listen to a podcast you save for walks. Read the book you want, not the one you think you should. Pleasure is not cheating; it is the mechanism.

When you slip, and you will

Slipping is part of keeping a resolution, not the opposite of it. What matters is whether the slip ends the attempt, and the danger is the all or nothing voice that says the year is ruined because of one Tuesday.

Treat the next day as a fresh start of its own, because by the research it is one. Monday works. So does the first of the month. You do not need to wait for next January.

Rest and renewal, a fresh start and hope: a day has ended, long live tomorrow!

Henri-Frédéric Amiel, Amiel's Journal

Keep it in sight

By the middle of February most resolutions are not abandoned so much as forgotten. The busy weeks come back and the intention slips out of view. Put it somewhere you will see it without trying: written on the first page of your diary, as a reminder at the time your plan says, or on your phone's Lock Screen, the screen you look at more than any other.

Some people want to devour in one day what they could barely digest in a lifetime.

Baltasar Gracián, The Art of Worldly Wisdom

Questions

Do New Year's resolutions actually work?

More often than their reputation suggests. In a 2002 study by John Norcross, 46 percent of people who made a New Year's resolution were still succeeding six months later, against 4 percent of people with the same goals who did not make one.

Why do most New Year's resolutions fail?

They are usually too big, worded as something to stop rather than something to do, and left without a plan for when and where. Many are not abandoned so much as forgotten once the busy weeks return.

How do I keep my New Year's resolution?

Make it something to do rather than something to avoid, start with a small version you could do on a bad day, decide in advance exactly when and where you will do it, choose a version you enjoy, and start again the next day after a slip.

What is the fresh start effect?

The finding by Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis that people are more likely to start working on a goal after a date that feels like a new beginning, such as the new year, a birthday, or the start of a week or month.

Is it too late to make a resolution after January?

No. Any date that feels like a new chapter, a Monday, the first of a month, a birthday, gives the same kind of fresh start. The best time to start is the next one you can find.
